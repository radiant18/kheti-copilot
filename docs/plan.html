The bet

What this product is, in one sentence

Government platforms hand farmers information. This turns information into a decision, with the money attached.

The thread you started with landed on the right instinct and the wrong scope. The instinct — stop building "ask me anything about farming" and start building "tell me what to do today" — is correct and is the whole basis of this plan. The scope, "Indian farmers," is where products like this die.

So the plan narrows hard: arecanut growers in the Dakshina Kannada and Uttara Kannada belt. One crop, one region, one language, roughly 20–30 households you can drive to. Everything below follows from that choice.

What to keep from the thread, and what to change

Reworking the original idea

Keep

  • The "Farm Today" screen as the whole product. One screen, a handful of actions, ranked. This is right and it is the hard part.
  • Profit as the spine. Framing advice as rupees rather than agronomy is what makes a farmer open the app twice.
  • Not competing with government portals on information. Correct, and the research below makes it more true than the thread knew.

Change

  • "Pick one crop" was advice; make it the architecture. A generic engine with arecanut configured in is still a generic engine. The rules here are arecanut rules — koleroga windows, chali grades, a 40-day Bordeaux cycle. That specificity is the moat.
  • Photo disease diagnosis is the wrong opening move. The thread ranks Crop Doctor as the easiest MVP. It is also the most crowded (Plantix owns it), needs a labelled arecanut dataset that does not really exist publicly, and for the disease that actually matters here — Yellow Leaf Disease — a correct diagnosis changes nothing, because there is no cure. Ship photo capture as evidence collection, not diagnosis.
  • Add the decision the thread missed entirely: which yard, which grade. This turns out to be where the money is. See below.
  • The LLM does not decide anything. The thread assumes an "AI decision engine." Make the engine deterministic rules; let the model translate and explain. A hallucinated fungicide dose is a destroyed crop.

Who is already standing here

Competitive landscape, 2026

This is a crowded category, and it is worth being blunt about that before writing more code. The relevant question is not "is anyone doing farm advisory" — many people are — but "is anyone giving an arecanut grower in Puttur a specific, priced instruction in Kannada."

A high-value perennial in a two-district coastal belt, in a language nobody has shipped in yet, is exactly the shape of thing a large player rationally skips.

Why arecanut, and why this belt

The wedge

Four things make arecanut in coastal Karnataka unusually good ground for this, and the fourth is the one that decided it.

  1. The crop is worth enough to be worth optimising. A three-acre mature garden yields roughly 27 quintals; at current Rashi prices that is around ₹13–14 lakh of gross revenue. A 5% improvement is real money, unlike on paddy.
  2. Two simultaneous crises create urgency. Yellow Leaf Disease is destroying mature gardens across Shivamogga and spreading; Karnataka has asked the Centre for ₹197 crore just to research it. Around 50 lakh people in the state depend on the crop.
  3. Prices are violent. Arecanut moved from about ₹58,000 to ₹39,000 per quintal inside a single season. Growers hold stock and guess.
  4. There is a large, unclaimed pricing inefficiency. ⤦

The strategic read: Bharat-VISTAAR will eventually cover Karnataka and Kannada, and when it does it will do weather, prices and schemes better than you can. It will not do "take your Rashi to Holalkere, not Puttur, it is ₹2,950 better after the lorry." Build toward the thing that stays yours.

The four decisions

Product definition

The entire product is one screen answering four questions, ranked by urgency and then by rupees at stake. If a feature does not serve one of these, it is out of scope.

Irrigation

Do I water today?

Cycle length by delivery method against 48-hour rainfall. Answering "no" is as valuable as answering "yes" — a skipped cycle on three acres is roughly ₹540 of diesel and pump time.

Koleroga spray window

When can I actually spray?

Phytophthora meadii takes 30–50% of a coastal crop in a bad monsoon, and Bordeaux mixture needs about six dry hours to set. The app derives dry hours from hourly forecast data and names the day. Portals publish rainfall; none of them say "Thursday, roughly nine dry hours."

Garden health

Is this Yellow Leaf Disease?

Deliberately not a diagnosis. Timestamped, geotagged photographs of yellowing fronds, building a per-block record. The decision it eventually informs is the expensive one: whether to replant into that block at all.

Selling

Which yard, which grade, which day?

Every mandi quoting your grade, ranked by what you actually pocket after transport, with the gap over the next-best yard stated in rupees. The one feature nobody else has.

The non-negotiable design constraints

  • Rules decide, the model explains. The engine is pure and deterministic. An LLM layer may translate a recommendation into Kannada or read it aloud; it may never originate one.
  • Every recommendation shows its arithmetic. "7 days since your last irrigation and only 4mm expected" earns trust in a way "AI recommends irrigation" never will.
  • Offline is the normal case, not the error case. Gardens are under canopy with patchy 4G. Cached inputs replayed through today's rules, clearly labelled as stale.
  • Kannada first. Not a translation layer added in month four.

One codebase, three targets

Architecture

You wanted a web app you can later package for Android and iOS. That works, but only if you make one decision correctly at the start, because retrofitting it is painful.

next build → web app on Vercel, API routes live MOBILE_BUILD=1 next build → static bundle in ./out npx cap sync → Android + iOS shells wrapping that bundle

The mobile shells have no server. They are served from capacitor://localhost, so a same-origin call to /api/weather resolves to nothing. Every network call therefore goes through one helper that prefixes NEXT_PUBLIC_API_BASE — empty on web, the deployed origin in the app. That single indirection is the whole trick, and it is already in the scaffold.

The second structural decision: the engine runs on the client. lib/engine/ is pure TypeScript with no I/O and no dependencies. It takes a farm profile, a weather window and a market view, and returns ranked recommendations. Because it is pure, it runs identically on the server, in the browser, and in the app with no signal at all — which is how a grower under canopy still gets an answer.

Why not React Native or Flutter

Because the web app is not a stepping stone here, it is a permanent channel. A grower's son shares a WhatsApp link; that has to open something useful without an install. Capacitor keeps one codebase across the link, the Play Store and the App Store. The cost is that anything needing deep native performance — heavy on-device vision, background location — gets awkward, and none of v1 needs that.

What ships when

Scope discipline

Hackathon build — already scaffolded and running

  • Farm profile: location, acreage, planting year, irrigation method, stock by grade
  • Live weather with derived dry hours per day
  • Deterministic rules: irrigation cycle, koleroga spray window, YLD photo prompt
  • Mandi ranking net of transport, with the gap over next-best in rupees
  • Cost book and season P&L
  • Offline-first, installable PWA, dark and light

Cut from v1, on purpose

Photo diagnosis (no dataset, no cure for the disease that matters). Marketplace or input sales (changes what the advice is for). Multi-crop (dilutes the rules). Sensors (different product). Scheme eligibility (the government does this well and it is not why anyone opens the app twice).

Roadmap

These run in order; each gate is a real stop

00now

Get the agronomy reviewed

This blocks everything and costs almost nothing. The thresholds in the engine — 40-day Bordeaux cover, six dry hours, a six-day sprinkler cycle, the humidity and temperature bands for koleroga — come from published practice, not from an agronomist reviewing your code.

  • Take the rules to ICAR-CPCRI or a Krishi Vigyan Kendra in Puttur or Sirsi
  • Ask CAMPCO how grading disputes actually get settled at the yard

Gate: do not show a spray recommendation to a real grower until someone qualified has signed off on it.

01wks 1–3

Ten farmers, Kannada, WhatsApp

Do not scale the app. Recruit ten growers you can physically visit, put the app in Kannada, and send each of them the day's plan as a WhatsApp message every morning at 6am — by hand if necessary.

  • Kannada UI via Bhashini; voice can wait
  • Log every recommendation and whether they acted on it
  • Server-side price history so trends become real

Gate: at least 4 of 10 open it unprompted in week three. If nobody does, the product is wrong, not the marketing.

02wks 4–8

Package and harden

Only now does mobile packaging earn its place — you have proof the daily plan is worth a notification.

  • cap add android, Play Store internal testing track
  • Push notifications at 6am; this is the actual retention mechanism
  • Accounts and sync, replacing localStorage
  • Kannada voice output for the daily plan — text is a barrier for older growers
  • Move off the Open-Meteo free tier

Gate: iOS only after Android retention holds. It doubles review overhead and the belt is overwhelmingly Android.

03wks 9–16

Deepen the sell engine

The defensible part. Everything else is replicable; this compounds with data you accumulate and nobody else has.

  • Grade-transition modelling: what better drying is worth, in rupees
  • Price forecasting from your own accumulated history plus arrivals volume
  • Hold-versus-sell with storage loss factored in
  • Aggregate anonymised yard data into a belt-level price view — the network effect

What to ask ten growers

Validation, before more code

Do not ask whether they would use an app; everyone says yes. Ask about last season, where the answers are checkable:

  • Where did you sell your last lot, and did you check any other yard's price first?
  • How did you decide it was Rashi and not Chippu? Who decided — you or the trader?
  • How many Bordeaux rounds did you do last monsoon? Did rain waste any of them?
  • What did you spend on the garden last year? (If they cannot answer, the cost book has a real job.)
  • Show them a screenshot: which line here would you actually have acted on?

The one number that matters from this: how many of the ten checked more than one mandi price before their last sale. If it is most of them, the sell engine is a convenience. If it is one or two, it is the business.

Honest risks

The things most likely to kill this

Highest

Wrong advice destroys a crop. A mistimed spray recommendation on a three-acre garden is a six-figure loss and the end of your reputation in a belt where everyone knows each other. Mitigation: deterministic rules only, agronomist sign-off, visible arithmetic, and conservative defaults that say "check with your KVK" rather than guessing.

High

Agmarknet is late, thin, or wrong. Yards report at different times, some skip days, variety spellings drift. A confident price recommendation built on a three-day-old quote is worse than no recommendation. Mitigation: always show the quote date, drop unrecognised varieties rather than guessing, and eventually crowdsource actual realised prices from your own users.

Medium

The commission agent relationship. Many growers do not choose their yard freely — they are tied to a trader by credit taken against the standing crop. Your arbitrage advice may be unusable for exactly the farmers who need it most. Find this out in week one, not month six; it may reframe the feature toward negotiating leverage rather than switching.

Medium

Bharat-VISTAAR expands. Karnataka and Kannada will arrive. Plan to be complementary — the thing that does the arithmetic on top of public information — rather than betting they stay away.

Medium

Nobody pays. Advisory has a long history of free. BharatAgri's paid subscription is the counter-evidence, and arecanut's economics are far better than the crops where free is the norm.

How it could make money

Business model, ranked by fit

Subscription, per garden, per season. ₹1,200–2,000 a year against a ₹13 lakh gross on three acres is a rounding error, and one good yard recommendation pays for it. BharatAgri has already demonstrated Indian farmers will pay a subscription for structured advisory. This is the clean model: your incentive stays aligned with the grower's, which is precisely what an input-selling advisory cannot claim.

Grower collectives and FPOs. Sell to the organisation, not the individual — one contract covers a few hundred gardens and solves distribution, which is the genuinely hard part.

What to refuse: commission on input sales, or any trader relationship that pays you for where the crop goes. The moment the sell recommendation has a kickback behind it, the only feature nobody else has becomes worthless.
